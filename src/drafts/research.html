Research

My research seeks to understand how the dynamic interplay of children's inner and outer worlds gives rise to the development of emotion, communication, and well-being.

To answer these questions I integrate developmental, affective, cognitive, and social neuroscience perspectives, using four complementary methods:

  • Computational approaches to annotate large datasets of naturalistic interaction and detect patterns in affect and communication.
  • Ecological momentary assessment — daylong audio recordings, head-mounted cameras, densely sampled caregiver surveys.
  • Experimental paradigms that translate descriptive findings into tests of causal mechanism.
  • Infant-friendly neurophysiology — functional near-infrared spectroscopy, pupillometry, eye gaze, and heart rate.

The ultimate goal of my work is to explain how thousands of small-scale, dynamic social interactions over the course of development give rise to our everyday emotional experience, in all of its richness and complexity.

Theme one

How do children experience and perceive affect across time?

Using densely sampled parental reports, I captured how young children's emotional experiences unfold day to day. Young infants showed more random and idiosyncratic patterns of emotion transitions than adults — abrupt swings from happy to fussy and back that are uncommon in adulthood. Over the first five years of life, children gradually converged onto a shared, organized, adult-like pattern (Nencheva et al., 2024, Affective Science).

Why do those transitions converge? One possibility is that children learn the statistical regularities in the emotion transitions of people around them. Measuring infants' pupillary responses to emotion transitions in the lab, I found that infants with similar emotional experiences at home processed those transitions similarly (Nencheva et al., 2026, JEP: LMC) — consistent with the idea that infants learn how emotions unfold by observing their caregivers. This line of work opens a potential link between caregiver mental health and children's emotional development.

Tracking others' affect also supports communication. Using dual-brain functional near-infrared spectroscopy alongside computer vision and speech processing, I found that caregivers' and infants' neural activity preceded fluctuations in valence and arousal by several seconds, and that this predictive processing was associated with greater caregiver-child neural coupling.

Theme two

How do affective dynamics interact with language learning?

Children with more mature emotion transitions have larger vocabularies and know more emotion labels. To understand that link, I combined dense sampling of children's affect with automated speech analysis of daylong audio recordings. When children displayed more positive valence and higher arousal, they also experienced richer communication with their caregivers — more vocalizations, more turn-taking — and they were more likely to know words used in positive, high-arousal contexts (Nencheva et al., Developmental Science). In a large corpus of transcribed interactions, words that elicit more laughter are learned earlier.

I also examine the affective information surrounding specific words in children's input. Analyzing a longitudinal corpus of head-mounted camera recordings with speech processing, computer vision, and affect classification, I found that canonical positive facial displays were rarely in infants' view — but when present, they co-occurred with positively valenced words. Linguistic expressions of positive valence were far more prevalent and robustly matched the valence of the words around them.

Building on these descriptive findings, recent experiments probe causal connections. Manipulating the affect displayed by an animated character, and manipulating preschoolers' and adults' own affective experiences, both yield preliminary evidence of better memory for words presented in positive, high-arousal contexts.

Theme three

How does the temporal structure of speech shape attention and learning?

Speech is one of the most salient early communicative signals. I investigate it through the lens of neural entrainment — the process by which the brain's rhythmic activity matches the dynamics of an external signal in moments of time-locked attention. I proposed that the affective, acoustic, and structural features common to infant-directed speech are specifically tailored to facilitate entrainment in the developing brain (Nencheva & Lew-Williams, 2022, Developmental Review).

Using computational methods to identify patterns in caregivers' pitch and pupillary measures of entrainment, I showed that toddlers learn novel words best when they are spoken in certain pitch contours (Nencheva, Piazza, & Lew-Williams, 2021, Developmental Science). Related work shows that the rich dimensionality of caregivers' communicative behavior is crucial for understanding early learning.

Looking ahead

Where the work is going

Understanding affective context through big-data approaches

During communication children receive many simultaneous cues — vocal tone, words, facial movement — that carry affective information. I am using machine-learning methods to uncover temporal patterns across these dimensions and to examine how they influence attention and learning, including in a large dataset of egocentric video spanning the first years of life.

Multi-pronged measures of children's internal states

Our understanding of children's internal states is constrained by reliance on parent report and the limited specificity of neurophysiological measures. I am combining measurement sources sensitive to different aspects and timescales of affective experience — mobile heart rate and respiration, and longer-timescale markers such as salivary cortisol.

Identifying causal influences in both directions

Large-scale descriptive studies reveal how affect and communication unfold in the real world; moving toward causal explanation requires integrating those insights into experimental manipulations and leveraging longitudinal data in naturalistic contexts.

Together, this work builds toward a central goal: to support children's learning and well-being by advancing our understanding of their everyday, dynamic experiences.
